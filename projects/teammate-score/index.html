Teammate Score

Enter a League of Legends Riot ID and get a statistical breakdown of how well your teammates performed across your last 50 games — scored against role-specific baselines, not just relative to the enemy team.

The problem

League has five roles, and each one has completely different performance expectations. A Support with a vision score of 60 is having a great game; a Midlaner with the same score is having an exceptional one. If you just compare raw stats across your teammates, the Support looks worse than they are — or the Jungler's kill participation looks inflated because Junglers are expected to be everywhere. I wanted a score that accounted for this, so I could actually tell whether a loss was because my teammates underperformed or because the enemy team just played better.

How the scoring works

For each teammate in each of your last 50 matches, the app pulls their stats from the Riot Games Match V5 API and computes a Z-score for every metric — vision score, gold per minute, kill participation, damage per minute — against a set of role-specific baselines: 

Z = (player_stat − μ_role) / σ_role

Those Z-scores are then combined with weights that reflect how important each metric is to winning, and mapped onto a 0–100 scale centered at 50 (average performance for that role). A teammate who scores 65 played noticeably better than a typical player in their position; one who scores 35 dragged their lane.

Beyond individual scores, the app also runs a team gap analysis that sums Z-scores across all four teammates for each metric. When the sum drops below −2.0 — two standard deviations below expected across the whole team — it flags a deficiency and explains what it means strategically. A vision gap in every game, for example, means you were consistently playing with worse map information than the enemy team, regardless of whether you won or lost those games.

Architecture

The backend is a Flask API deployed on Render. It handles Riot API calls with rate-limit backoff, runs the statistical analysis, and caches results in Upstash Redis so that a repeat lookup for the same player returns instantly instead of re-fetching 50 match records. Match fetching is parallelized across eight threads, which cuts the cold-start time from roughly 50 seconds (sequential) to about 10.

The frontend is a React app deployed on Vercel. It kicks off an async job, polls the backend for progress, and renders the results — per-game teammate scores, aggregate stats, and consistency metrics across the match history — once the analysis completes.

What I took from it

The main thing this project taught me is how much the frame matters when you're evaluating performance. Raw numbers feel objective, but a Support's raw vision score and a Midlaner's raw vision score aren't measuring the same thing. Getting the normalization right meant thinking carefully about what "good" actually means per role, not just finding a formula that produced a number.

On the engineering side, managing an async job across a polling frontend and a threaded backend was a useful exercise in designing for latency — you want the user to see something meaningful while 50 API calls are in flight, not just a spinner.
