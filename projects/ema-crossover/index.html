EMA crossover momentum strategy

A long/short EMA(20)/EMA(50) crossover strategy backtested across 10 years of intraday AAPL data. The system resamples 1-minute bars to any timeframe, enforces a market-hours filter, models transaction costs per fill, and outputs a full trade log and visualization suite.

The strategy

EMA crossover is one of the most studied momentum signals: go long when the fast exponential moving average crosses above the slow one, go short when it crosses below. The strategy is always in the market — there is no cash period — which makes it a clean test of whether the signal has any forecasting power at all, because there is nowhere to hide in flat markets.

I chose EMA(20) and EMA(50) on weekly bars resampled from a 1-minute AAPL feed. The weekly timeframe removes most of the intraday microstructure noise while still generating enough crossovers to measure over a decade.

Implementation

The strategy is a single self-contained Python file. It reads a 1-minute OHLCV CSV, resamples it to the target timeframe using pandas resample(), computes the two EMAs on close prices, generates long/short signals at each crossover, and simulates trade execution bar by bar. Changing the timeframe — from 1-minute to weekly — requires only changing one string in __main__.

A market-hours filter masks signals outside 09:30–16:00 Eastern. Any position open at the last valid bar of a session is force-closed at that bar's close, eliminating overnight exposure. This made the cost accounting cleaner and the results more realistic.

Cost modelling

Each fill incurs $0.01 slippage and $0.005 commission, giving a $0.03 round-trip cost. With 46 trades over the backtest period, total costs were $1.38, dragging net return by 0.75 percentage points. The system writes separate gross and net trade books so cost impact is always visible.

Results (weekly bars, 10-year AAPL)

The strategy produced a 20.5% total gross return (1.96% annualized, Sharpe 0.29) with a maximum drawdown of 11.0%. Buy-and-hold over the same period returned 639% — the crossover signal substantially underperforms passive exposure to a secular growth stock. The more useful comparison is the drawdown: the strategy's 11% max drawdown against buy-and-hold's 38.6% shows the signal does provide some downside protection via its short leg, even if it sacrifices most of the upside.

Visualization

A companion script reads the six output CSVs and produces charts: the price series with EMA overlays and trade entry/exit markers, the equity curve gross vs net vs buy-and-hold, and the per-trade profit distribution. The visualizer is separate from the strategy engine so the charting code never touches live data.

What I took from it

The most useful lesson was how little a popular signal can actually do on a stock that moved 640% in a decade: the crossover's mean-reversion instincts actively fight the trend that makes AAPL interesting to own. That is not a flaw in the implementation — it is the correct answer, and getting the infrastructure right made the answer legible. The project also reinforced the value of separating the strategy engine from the reporting layer: the visualizer required zero changes to the strategy code, which meant I could iterate on charts without worrying about accidentally introducing a bug in the signal logic.
