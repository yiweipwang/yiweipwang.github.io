Tilings of hexagonal boards

What happens to the classic "tile a board, get Fibonacci" result when the board is made of hexagons instead of squares? It turns out a different — and older — sequence shows up.

Year
2023
Type
Independent research
Tools
Pen & paper, Python, LaTeX
Published
OEIS A365352

The question

A well-known introductory result in combinatorics says that the number of ways to tile a 1×n strip of squares with squares and dominoes is the Fibonacci number F(n+1). The squares version is thoroughly studied; I wanted to know how the structure changes when the underlying board is a strip of hexagonal cells, where each cell touches its neighbors along edges in a staggered, two-row pattern rather than in a single line.

The approach

I enumerated tilings of small hexagonal strips by hand to get trustworthy initial terms, then wrote a short Python brute-forcer to extend the sequence far enough to conjecture a pattern. The counts didn't match Fibonacci — but they satisfied a clean third-order recurrence. The sequence turned out to be governed by Narayana's cows, a(n) = a(n−1) + a(n−3), a recurrence first posed as a 14th-century cattle-breeding puzzle. The geometry explains the lag: on the hexagonal strip, one family of tile placements commits cells three steps ahead instead of two, which is exactly what shifts the Fibonacci-style recurrence from order 2 to order 3.

From there I proved the recurrence directly with a case analysis on how the leftmost cell of the strip can be covered, mirroring the standard Fibonacci tiling proof but adapted to hexagonal adjacency.

The result

The sequence was new to the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences and is now published as A365352, with the recurrence, initial terms, and tiling interpretation on record. The OEIS is the standard reference database for integer sequences, so the result is citable and independently checkable — which is my favorite property a project can have.

What I took from it

Two things. First, brute force is a legitimate research instrument: the Python enumerator didn't prove anything, but it told me exactly what to try to prove. Second, recurrences are a place where math and programming are the same activity — the case analysis in the proof and the branching in the enumerator are literally the same tree.
