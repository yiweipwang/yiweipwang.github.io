Minesweeper, with an opponent

The classic game, plus the three things the classic game never gives you: a tutorial that teaches the logic, a hint when you're stuck, and an opponent that plays the same board against you.

Year
2024
Type
Game + AI systems
Stack
Java, JavaFX
Code
GitHub

Why this project

Minesweeper looks like a toy but contains two genuinely interesting computer-science problems: efficiently revealing connected regions of the board, and reasoning about where the mines probably are given incomplete information. I wanted to solve both well enough that the program could not just play the game, but teach it and compete at it.

How it works

The flood reveal — what happens when you click an empty cell and a whole region opens up — is a breadth-first search over the grid, expanding from the clicked cell until it reaches the numbered frontier. BFS keeps the reveal correct and instant even on large boards, and the same traversal doubles as the win-condition check.

The hint engine and the AI opponent share a risk model. For each unopened cell on the frontier, the solver builds a tree of consistent mine assignments under the visible number constraints and uses it to estimate each cell's mine probability. The hint system surfaces the lowest-risk cell to the player; the opponent simply takes it. When no safe deduction exists — the situations where human players guess — the opponent guesses by the same probabilities, which makes its play feel honest rather than psychic.

The tutorial mode walks a new player through those same deductions on curated boards, highlighting which numbers force which conclusions, so the "AI" and the teaching material are one codebase rather than two.

What I took from it

Designing the opponent forced a clean separation between game state, inference, and UI — the solver knows nothing about JavaFX, which made it testable with plain unit tests. It was also a lesson in product thinking: the hint feature and the opponent are the same algorithm wearing two different interfaces.
